01 THE RISK OF ONE DAY

We spend a lot of our lives preparing for the future.

We work hard. We make sacrifices. We save. We invest. We plan.

And often, we tell ourselves that one day, when we have enough time, enough money or enough freedom, we’ll finally do the things we really want to do.

But there’s a risk in always waiting for one day.

Don’t save your healthiest dreams for your oldest body.

That doesn’t mean we should stop planning for tomorrow.

Far from it.

Planning for the future matters. Financial security matters. Having choices matters.

But so does today.

Because a good life isn’t simply about accumulating as much as we can. It’s about making thoughtful choices about how we use what we have, while we have it.

And what we have is much more than money.

We have time.

We have health.

We have relationships.

We have abilities, opportunities and experiences.

Some of those things may grow as we get older. Others become harder to recover once they’re gone.

That’s why the question isn’t simply, “How much will I have?”

It’s also, “What do I want my money to help me do?”

That’s an important distinction.

02 LIFE IS THE OUTCOME

Money is valuable because of the choices it can create. It can provide security. It can give us independence. It can help us care for the people we love. And it can give us the freedom to spend more of our lives doing the things that matter to us.

But money is the tool.

Life is the
outcome.

And that outcome will look different for every one of us.

For one person, living well might mean travelling the world.

For someone else, it might mean having more time with family.

It might mean starting something new. Giving something back. Working less. Working longer because you love what you do. Learning something. Creating something. Or simply having the confidence to know that you’ll be okay.

There is no universal definition of a life well lived.

And there certainly isn’t a single dollar figure that defines it.

We can easily look at someone who has more and assume they have a better life.

The bigger home.
The better car.
The larger super balance.

But we’re only seeing one part of their story.

Having more does not guarantee happiness, just as having less does not make a life less meaningful.

We all start from different places. We face different challenges. We value different things.

It’s not about what you have. It’s about what you do with what you have.
03 NOW AND LATER

That idea matters when we think about retirement, too.

Because retirement planning can easily become a conversation about numbers.

How much have I accumulated?
How much more do I need?
What income will I have?

They’re important questions.

But they’re not the only questions.

We should also be asking:

What kind of life am I actually trying to fund?

Because reaching retirement with the biggest possible balance shouldn’t be the objective if getting there meant continually postponing the things that mattered most.

But the opposite isn’t particularly wise either.

Living entirely for today and leaving tomorrow’s problems to our future selves isn’t freedom. It’s simply moving the problem from one point in our life to another.

The real challenge is finding the balance between the two.

And importantly, that balance is personal.

It shouldn’t be determined by what somebody else is doing.

It shouldn’t be based on what looks successful from the outside.

And it shouldn’t be dictated by an arbitrary number that tells us when we finally have “enough”.

It should reflect the life we want to live.

That will change over time.

What matters to us at 30 may be very different at 50.

And what matters at 70 may be different again.

Our money should adapt to those changes, not dictate them.

04 A LIFE WELL LIVED

There’s another trap worth avoiding too.

We shouldn’t turn living well into another exercise in optimisation.

Not every minute needs to be productive.
Not every opportunity needs to be seized.
Not every dollar needs to generate a return.

Sometimes an afternoon doing very little with people we care about is a remarkably good use of both time and money.

The point isn’t to squeeze absolutely everything we can out of life.

It’s simply to make sure we don’t spend so much time preparing to live that we forget to actually live.

There is no perfect formula.

There’s no magic number that suddenly gives us permission to enjoy ourselves.

And there’s no age when life officially begins.

The goal isn’t to die with the most. Nor is it to spend everything today.

It’s to make thoughtful choices.
To enjoy what we can while we can.
To look after the people we care about.
And to give our future selves the same opportunity.

Because ultimately, financial wellbeing isn’t just about how much money we accumulate.

It’s about what that money allows us to do.

A FINAL THOUGHT

And a life well lived isn’t measured
by what we leave sitting in an account.

It’s measured by what
we make of the
life we have.
